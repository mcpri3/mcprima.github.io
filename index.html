From individual movement to the connectivity of whole landscapes

I am a postdoctoral researcher in spatial and movement ecology at the Alpine Ecology Lab (LECA), in France (Grenoble). I develop methods to understand, quantify and improve landscape connectivity across scales, and apply them to biodiversity conservation and protected area policy.

I am also a mum of two amazing girls, who keep me busy in my spare time (but totally worth it!)

Research

My work sits between theoretical ecology, methodological development and conservation applications. I combine mechanistic approaches, based on individual movement behaviour, with phenomenological ones such as niche models, to link local movement mechanisms to large-scale connectivity patterns. I use different types of datasets whether they are real (e.g., occurrences, GPS tracking) or simulated, and I mostly work on vertebrate species (but do some incursions into the invertebrate world).

Movement ecology and landscape connectivity methods

Many research results come from applying modelling tools, which have their own limitations that are often left unquantified. I particularly enjoy quantifying these limitations and finding solutions to them, in order to improve the toolkit available to spatial ecologists. Some examples:

  • Recommendations to correct statistical biases from autocorrelated movement data (Link)
  • Building on the advantages of network and circuit theories, I developed a framework to evaluate simultaneously landscape and protected area connectivity for multiple species (Link)
  • Spatial network evaluation: simple network models poorly reproduce real connectivity in heterogeneous or dynamic landscapes - case studies with simulated and Alpine ibex movement data in France (Link), and woodland caribou in Canada (Link)

Connectivity of protected areas (PA) in hexagonal France (HorizonEurope NaturaConnect and MODIC projects)

Quantifying PA connectivity is of utmost importance, both to strategically meet current conservation targets (e.g., 30x30), and to understand the role of PA networks under future environmental changes. Working in collaboration with the OFB and the European NaturaConnect network, we mapped the distribution of ecological continuities for about 400 species, identifying large connectivity gaps for the most threatened species (i.e., including invertebrates), and projected future PA connectivity under climate and land-use change scenarios. More details here and here.

Pressures, policy and ecosystem services (Biodiversa+ Reconnect project)

Biodiversity provides many services to humans, yet it is under strong human pressure. My past and ongoing work seek to understand where species are most vulnerable to these pressures, how ecosystem services are affected along different land-use gradients, and whether positive human action, such as connectivity restoration, can help restore these services. This work has led to:

  • An extensive mapping of human pressure distribution in hexagonal France (Link), and overseas territories (Guadeloupe, La Réunion)
  • An extensive mapping of biodiversity vulnerability to human pressures (Link)
  • An evaluation of the Natura 2000 policy (Link)
  • Connectivity-based models of ecosystem services and restoration scenarios, in virtual landscapes (Link) and in the Grenoble region (Link)

AlpiConnect project (OFB-LECA partnership, 2026-2028)

This ongoing research project aims to combine different data types with available knowledge to map landscape connectivity for emblematic vertebrate species in French Alpine regions (the Alps and the Pyrenees), to identify common drivers of the connectivity across species and movement processes and, to work toward integrating this knowledge into public policy.
